01

The prototype

A companion that can hold the pause you agreed to.

The care system grew through repeated refinement in Mori, Z’s personal companion prototype. It combines contextual conversation with explicit care state: a reminder can become a firmer boundary, then a stop on further task work.

A stop stays in place until the person explicitly confirms the relevant action is complete, unless routine work stops are disabled. “I’ll take a break” and “I took a break” mean different things. Care also means accepting that confirmation and not asking for the same action again.

  1. 01 /

    Start with a reminder.

    The first tier brings the agreed pause into the conversation. The companion stays with the work context and gives a clear, manageable action rather than a generic motivational message.

  2. 02 /

    Hold a firmer boundary.

    Higher tiers make the boundary explicit. With routine care stops enabled, the companion can decline to continue task work and ask for confirmation that the break happened.

  3. 03 /

    Recognize the return.

    Explicit completion clears the matching care boundary. The companion should resume without a lecture. Recognizing ordinary confirmation language and avoiding stale reminders remain part of ongoing testing.

You choose whether care can stop the work. The prototype has a switch for routine care stops. Turning it off leaves reminders and separate safety rules in place. The current opt-out lasts for the running companion session and resets when its service restarts. It is not a device lock or a medical safeguard.
What works now, and what are we still testing?

The personal companion prototype implements tiered care boundaries, explicit completion handling and a routine-work-stop switch. It is under testing in an ongoing founder-led setting. This is working implementation experience, not evidence of effectiveness across a user population.

Paperkinn’s MCP packaging, broader compatibility and external user trials are still in development; this is not a released feature set. MCP state indexing and cognitive-load scaffolding are the product direction. No public download is offered here, and we make no clinical or validated productivity claims.

02

Why another tool?

It has to earn its place beside your existing tools.

Good alternatives already exist. Tiimo offers visual planning and AI task breakdown. Focusmate and Flow Club provide human presence and accountability. Finch makes self-care companion-like through a virtual pet.

The closest overlaps go further: Indy by Shimmer offers goal-aware conversational support, Leantime connects project context to Claude through MCP, and Claude Code already has memory and session tools. Paperkinn brings implementation experience with tiered boundaries, explicit completion and optional work stops to that care interaction. We still need to establish whether developers prefer it to their existing tools.

The next proof is use, not a bigger feature list. We need to test whether context-sensitive support feels useful rather than intrusive, whether checkpoints are accurate, and whether developers choose it again over their existing setup. More time in conversation is not our definition of success. These are evaluation goals, not results.
03

Grounded in research

A working prototype. More people to learn from.

Interviews with developers with ADHD and autism describe difficulties with task initiation, planning and returning to context. First-person accounts also emphasize autonomy: support needs to fit the person using it.

These experiences are not universal across ADHD or neurodivergence. The research below motivates this direction. The founder-led prototype gives us implementation experience, but neither establishes broader product effectiveness.

  • Developer experiences with ADHD and autism · 2024 qualitative preprint; 25 neurodivergent participants and five comparators. A small study, not a market estimate.
  • What developers with ADHD want you to know · Two engineers’ accounts, published by Stack Overflow in 2023.
  • Indy’s ADHD companion and Leantime’s MCP integration · Official descriptions of close alternatives, not independent evidence of efficacy.
  • Claude Code’s existing memory features · The native baseline an additional tool must justify itself against.
What would I need to share?

For an initial research conversation, a description of a recent interruption or difficult restart is enough. Please don’t email source code, credentials, employer-confidential information or medical records.

Before any prototype trial, the invitation should explain exactly what the plugin reads, stores and sends, and how to remove that data. Local-only storage and other privacy controls are not claims we make about the current prototype.

For investors & collaborators

A focused starting point. A measurable next step.

Our initial focus is developers who already use AI coding companions and want support with starting, staying with a task and stopping without losing context.

The thesis is a companion-style care layer within active coding work. Native agent tools make this practical to explore, and easy to imitate. The offering must earn preference through useful, reliable support, not a protocol or a friendly voice alone.

We have a working personal companion prototype under testing, including tiered care boundaries and optional work stops. Demand, willingness to pay and repeat use beyond that setting are not yet established. The next milestone is a user trial against native agent setups and existing companion or planning tools, measuring usefulness, unwanted interruptions and voluntary return.
